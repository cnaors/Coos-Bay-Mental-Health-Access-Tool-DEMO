What actually happens when you call?

988: a trained counselor answers, usually within a minute. They listen, talk things through, and help you make a plan. You can stay anonymous and hang up any time. Emergency services get involved only in the rare case where someone is in immediate danger and can't stay safe — the vast majority of calls are just a conversation.

The county line: a local crisis worker answers. They can talk you through it, set up a next-day appointment, or send the mobile crisis team out — and they know the local system, so they connect you to real follow-up.

Either way: free, and calling about someone else is normal.

Good tools online

Reputable self-checks, plain-language education, and directories to use between appointments.

Wondering if what you're feeling is depression or anxiety? The free screening tools at screening.mhanational.org (Mental Health America) are anonymous, clinically validated self-tests that take about 5 minutes, no sign-up. The results page explains what your score suggests and what kind of help fits — a screen points the way; only a provider can diagnose.

Want to understand a condition in plain words? nami.org (National Alliance on Mental Illness) explains conditions and treatments for patients and families, and nimh.nih.gov (the National Institute of Mental Health) carries the research-backed version of the same topics.

PTSD Coach is a free, evidence-based app from the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs with symptom tracking and coping tools you can use between appointments — built for veterans and service members and open to anyone, first responders included. Search "PTSD Coach" in your phone's app store.

Psychology Today's therapist finder (psychologytoday.com) is the biggest national directory, searchable by insurance and by telehealth. Profiles are self-written, so confirm Oregon licensure and your insurance when you call.

How to ask someone if they're thinking about suicide

Ask directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Research shows asking does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior — talking openly may actually reduce them (NIMH).

The five research-backed steps: Ask plainly and listen without judgment. Be there. Help keep them safe — ask if they have a plan, and put distance between them and anything they could use. Help them connect — call or text 988 together. Follow up — a check-in call in the days after genuinely lowers risk.

What if they refuse help?

You can't force an adult into care, and pushing hard often backfires. What works: keep the relationship open, name what you see without judgment ("I've noticed you're not sleeping — I'm worried"), and offer specific small next steps rather than "get help."

Call 988 or 541-266-6800 yourself for coaching on your exact situation. Immediate danger they won't stay safe from: 911. And your own limits are real — the NAMI family group exists because carrying this alone breaks people.

Can I share patient information with these services?
The HIPAA rules for crisis referrals, in plain language — referrals for care are permitted, and so are imminent-danger calls.

Referrals for care — yes

HIPAA permits sharing patient information without a signed authorization when it is for treatment, and that includes referrals and coordinating care with another provider (45 CFR 164.506). Calling mobile crisis or the county line about a patient to arrange care is a treatment disclosure.

Serious danger — yes

When you believe in good faith that a patient poses a serious and imminent threat to themselves or someone else, HIPAA lets you alert anyone reasonably able to lessen that threat — the crisis team, police, or family (45 CFR 164.512(j)).

How the crisis lines handle what you share

988 and the county crisis line are confidential services. 988's platform is secured to HIPAA Security Rule standards, callers can stay anonymous, and identifiable information stays inside the service except at imminent risk of harm or where the law requires disclosure. The county line is run by Coos Health & Wellness, itself a covered healthcare provider.

SAFE Project advocates work differently. They are community advocates rather than a HIPAA covered entity, and Oregon law protects what survivors tell them. Information flows to the advocate with the patient's OK, and back to you only with the survivor's consent.

Rule of thumb: ask the patient's permission when you can — it is fastest and cleanest. Lean on the treatment and imminent-danger provisions when you can't. Share the minimum needed for anything beyond treatment, and document what you shared and why.

Sources: HHS — HIPAA & mental health information · 45 CFR 164.512 · 988 confidentiality policy. Your facility's privacy officer and policies control.

How to build these services into your care
Warm handoffs, safety planning, and follow-up — the steps that raise the odds your patient actually connects.

Make it a warm handoff

Call the service with the patient in the room, introduce them by name, and stay until the connection is made. A warm handoff connects far more patients than a phone number on a discharge sheet.

Before discharge

Run a brief safety plan (Stanley-Brown or your facility's tool), counsel on lethal means, and ask about the county's crisis respite beds — referrals come through Coos Health & Wellness or the Bay Area ED, and patients rarely know to ask.

After discharge

Brief caring contacts — a follow-up call or message in the days after a crisis — measurably lower repeat-crisis and suicide risk. Schedule the follow-up before the patient leaves, and document who you called and what was arranged so the next shift can pick it up.

Sexual assault patient — exam rights, evidence windows, advocate, reporting
Covers the full pathway — what Oregon guarantees the patient and what the law requires of you.

1. Medical needs first. Urgent care always precedes forensic care (Oregon SATF sequence).

2. Call the advocate — it's the law. ORS 147.404 requires medical staff to notify an advocate for sexual violence patients. Locally that's SAFE Project, 541-756-7000, 24/7 to the bedside. The patient chooses whether to use them. Patients also have the right to a support person (ORS 147.425).

3. Never "too late" for an exam. SATF: medical-forensic exams are possible for days after an assault (their guidance says up to ~15 days depending on circumstances). Oregon's SAVE Fund pays for SAFE Kit exams within 120 hours and non-kit exams within 168 hours. Time-critical meds: HIV nPEP within 72 hours; emergency contraception 72–120 hours.

4. Evidence preservation (if the patient may want collection): avoid showering, changing clothes, eating/drinking, urinating where feasible — and a patient who already showered can still have evidence collected. Oral swabs before food or drink.

5. The patient's rights (ORS 147.397): exam at no cost whether or not police are involved; anonymous, identity-protected kits are allowed; providers billing the SAVE Fund may not balance-bill the patient; law enforcement must keep kits at least 60 years.

6. Reporting nuance: an adult's sexual assault is NOT automatically police-reportable in Oregon — that's the patient's choice. The separate injury-reporting duty (ORS 146.750: physicians, PAs, RNs must report non-accidental physical injuries, historically framed as weapon or serious physical injuries) can still apply — follow your facility policy, and route minors under ORS 419B instead.

7. After the ED: SAFE advocates handle confidential shelter placement and safety planning. ODHS TA-DVS grants up to $3,200 for safety costs (moving, locks, deposits). Oregon DOJ's Address Confidentiality Program gives a substitute mailing address.

Protocols & standards: oregonsatf.org/medical-forensic (Oregon's medical-forensic guidelines & 2025 SAFE Kit companion) · patient-facing sequence at campusreportingoptions.org/medical-care · SAVE Fund details: doj.state.or.us

Non-fatal strangulation — treat it as a lethality red flag
Explains why strangulation is the single strongest predictor that a DV patient will later be killed.

Why it changes everything: prior non-fatal strangulation is associated with roughly 7.5× higher odds of later homicide by the same partner (Glass et al. 2008, per the Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention), and 43% of women killed by partners experienced it first. Unconsciousness can occur in seconds — and life-threatening injury can be present with zero visible marks (Oregon SATF's own SAFE Kit guidance says the same).

So: every strangulation disclosure gets a medical evaluation, not just a visual check. Since August 2023, Oregon's SAVE Fund pays for strangulation (SKIT) exams even when no sexual assault occurred.

And: connect the patient with SAFE (541-756-7000) before discharge — strangulation is exactly the case where safety planning saves a life.

Clinical resources: Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention

Behavioral health crisis — holds, transport, and placement
Covers who can place a hold, where patients go, and the respite beds nobody mentions.

Peace officer custody (ORS 426.228): an officer may take custody on probable cause the person is dangerous to self or others and needs immediate care. Destination: the nearest OHA-approved facility — locally, Bay Area Hospital's ED — where a licensed independent practitioner must examine the person immediately. If emergency care isn't indicated, the person cannot be retained.

Director's hold (ORS 426.233): the county mental health director (Coos Health & Wellness, 541-266-6700) can direct an officer to take custody or authorize nonhospital placement. When the director reports imminent danger, the officer shall take custody. Emergency admission proceeds under ORS 426.232.

Not hold-level? Mobile crisis (541-266-6800) can respond on scene, and the county's crisis respite beds (about 5–7 day stays) take referrals from CHW clinicians and the Bay Area ED — raise it before discharge; patients rarely know to ask.

Withdrawal risk: alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be lethal — route to Adapt's medical detox (541-492-0222, Rapid Access walk-in entry), never "sleep it off at home."

Reference: OHA civil commitment · ORS 426.228

Child with suspicious injuries — Karly's Law
Explains the 48-hour exam requirement and who Coos County's designated medical professional is.

The rule (ORS 419B.022–.024): a child with suspicious physical injuries in a child-abuse investigation must be seen by a trained designated medical professional within 48 hours, and the injuries must be photographed.

"Suspicious injuries" include: burns or scalds; extensive bruising; any bruising or swelling of the head, neck, or face; any fracture under age 3; multiple fractures at any age; unconsciousness; multiple injuries of different types.

In Coos County: the DMP is Dr. Jenni DeLeon at the Kids' HOPE Center (541-266-8806, 1925 Thompson Rd, Coos Bay) — forensic interviews, exams, advocacy, no cost to families. Report first: 855-503-7233 (ODHS, 24/7) or law enforcement.

Official summary: Oregon DOJ — Karly's Law

Elder & vulnerable adult abuse — the law and the signs
Covers who must report, how fast, and which signs justify the call.

The duty: ORS 124.060 — officials including physicians, nurses, EMS providers, and firefighters with reasonable cause to believe a person 65+ has been abused must report; ORS 124.065 requires the oral report immediately. ODHS extends the same intake to vulnerable adults 18+.

Where: 855-503-7233 (ODHS, 24/7) or local law enforcement. Nursing facility residents: 877-280-4555.

Signs that warrant a call (ACL)

  • Bruises, pressure marks, broken bones, burns — especially unexplained
  • Sudden withdrawal from activities, unusual depression, change in alertness
  • Bedsores, poor hygiene, unattended medical needs, unusual weight loss
  • Sudden changes in finances
  • Belittling or controlling caregiver behavior; tense caregiver relationship

One sign alone doesn't prove abuse — your job is to alert, not to verify.

Suspected child abuse — who must report, the law, and how

The law: ORS 419B.010 requires any "public or private official" with reasonable cause to believe a child has been abused to report immediately. The reporter list (ORS 419B.005) includes nurses, physicians, EMS providers and firefighters, school employees including higher-ed, therapists, clergy, attorneys, and more. Failure to report is a Class A violation.

Three things people get wrong: You don't need proof — reasonable suspicion is the standard, and investigating isn't your job. The duty is personal — telling your supervisor does not satisfy it. And it applies even if you learned of the abuse outside your job (ODHS: "regardless of whether or not you gained your knowledge… in your official capacity").

How: call 855-503-7233 (24/7) or local law enforcement — one agency is enough. Immediate danger: 911 first. In Coos County, cases route to the Kids' HOPE Center.

On the child's body

  • Injuries to the back, neck, ears, buttocks, or genitals — locations less typical of play
  • Any bruise on a baby under 4 months; mouth injuries in infants
  • Injuries with no explanation, or one that doesn't fit
  • Untreated medical, dental, or vision needs; poor growth without medical cause

In the child's behavior

  • Fear of a parent or not wanting to go home; cowering around a caregiver
  • Regularly running away, constant fatigue, stealing or hoarding food
  • Losing skills they had (like new bed-wetting); self-injury or talk of suicide
  • Direct statements about abuse — always taken seriously

In the caregiver's behavior

  • Disregard for the child's feelings or needs
  • Describing the child as bad, evil, worthless — or in sexual terms

ODHS caution: any single sign may or may not mean abuse — consider what's typical for that child, family, and culture. When in doubt, call and let the screeners decide.

When you hand someone this guide

Most people try the number they're given, but only about a third end up getting help — the drop-off is dead ends and discouragement. Beat those odds: pick one specific number, tell them what will happen when they call, and check back in a few days. "Did you get through?" is the highest-value question in this guide.

No insurance — what a visit costs at each place

These are the local places where mental health care costs the least without insurance. Start at the top — the county clinic is free or nearly free for most people. Tap any place to see what you'd pay and how to set it up.

County mental health clinic — Coos Bay
Coos Health & Wellness · counseling & psychiatry, $0–$10 a visit at most lower incomes
The county's walk-in mental health clinic. Your income sets your fee, and for most lower incomes the fee is $0.

What a visit costs at your income

  • Single person earning under about $1,835 a month — $0
  • Up to about $1,995 a month — $5
  • Up to about $2,330 a month — $8
  • Up to about $2,660 a month — $10
  • Above that, the full fee applies. The limits rise with household size — a family of four pays $0 under about $3,795 a month.

These fees cover their mental health services — therapy, psychiatric care, and youth programs. The first visit has a $10 minimum, and no one is refused services for being unable to pay.

How to set it up

Call 541-266-6700 or walk in Monday–Friday 8:00–4:30 at 281 LaClair St, Coos Bay. The business office asks your household size and monthly income, and you show proof — a pay stub, last year's taxes, or a benefits letter. That sets your fee, and it adjusts if your situation changes.

Source: CHW fee schedule · checked August 12, 2026.

Hospital & clinic — Bandon
Southern Coos Hospital · care is free at lower incomes
The Bandon hospital and its clinic, which offers counseling. Below about $2,660 a month for one person, your care costs nothing.

How much of your bill is covered

  • Income at or below about $2,660 a month for one person (200% of the federal poverty level) — the full bill
  • Somewhat above that (201–300%) — 75% of the bill
  • Higher still (up to 400%) — 25% of the bill
  • Any income — if your medical bills add up to more than 10% of your yearly income, you may qualify anyway

How to apply

Call Patient Financial Services at 541-347-2426, or visit the office at 900 11th St SE, Bandon. You fill out an application and include last year's tax return and recent pay stubs. They answer within 30 days, and an approval covers you for 12 months.

Source: Southern Coos financial assistance policy · checked August 12, 2026.

Hospital — Coos Bay
Bay Area Hospital · 25% to 100% taken off your bill
The Coos Bay hospital, including its psychiatric unit. Discounts depend on income and family size, and people with insurance can qualify too.

Depending on your income and family size, 25% to 100% of your hospital bill is written off. If your income is too high for that, there is still a discount for the uninsured, a discount for paying promptly, and payment plans.

How to apply

Call Patient Accounts at 541-266-6098, ask at check-in, or apply online; forms are available in Spanish. You list household size and income and attach proof.

Free help with the application: Dollar For, a nonprofit, fills out and files hospital assistance applications for you at no charge — and they work with Bay Area Hospital.

Source: Bay Area Hospital financial assistance · checked August 12, 2026.

Mental health & addiction clinic — North Bend · Bandon
Adapt · counseling & addiction treatment, priced by your income
Therapy and substance use treatment. Your fee slides with your income — including if you have no income at all.

Your fee is set from your household size and income, and no one is turned away for being unable to pay. If you have no income, a form you sign yourself takes the place of pay stubs, so missing paperwork doesn't stop you.

How to set it up

Ask for the sliding fee application when you schedule at 541-751-0357. For cost questions, their billing office is 541-229-8958.

Community health center — Coos Bay · North Bend
Waterfall · income discounts on counseling & psychiatry — call for your price
A community health center with counseling, psychiatry, and primary care. Fees are discounted by income; a phone call gets you your number.

Waterfall discounts care for every patient based on income and family size — it's a requirement of their federal funding. They don't post the amounts, so the price for your income takes one phone call to learn.

What to say when you call

Call 541-435-7000 and say: "I'd like to apply for the sliding fee discount — what would my first behavioral health visit cost at my income?" Bring proof of income to your first visit.

Primary care clinic — Coos Bay · Bandon · Coquille · Myrtle Point
North Bend Medical Center · ask for a price before you book
A primary care clinic with counselors and a psychiatric nurse practitioner. They don't post costs, so settle the price first.

Before booking, call the Patient Access team at 541-267-5151, ext. 1281 and ask two things: what a first visit costs if you're paying for yourself, and whether any discount or payment plan applies to you. Three more ways to keep costs down

Open Path Collective connects people without usable insurance (household income under $100,000) to licensed therapists — local and online — for $40–$70 a session after a one-time $65 membership: openpathcollective.org.

Your regular doctor can start and manage many mental health medications at a normal office-visit price — usually the cheapest way to get a prescriber. And at the pharmacy, ask for the GoodRx price on any prescription: generic sertraline, for example, runs about $31 a month at full price and closer to $10 with the free coupon.

Free, always: 988, the county crisis line, the Oregon Warmline, YouthLine, NAMI support groups, and SAFE Project services cost nothing.

OHP — free care, and how to get it

Not enrolled? You may qualify.

The Oregon Health Plan covers mental health care with no copay, and enrollment is open year-round. Apply at one.oregon.gov — most people finish the application in under an hour — or call 800-699-9075, weekdays 7–6, and they walk you through it. Free in-person application help is available at ODHS offices and community partners.

Already on OHP?

Your local plan is Advanced Health, and you never need a referral for mental health care — call any provider marked OHP in the directory directly. Member services: 800-264-0014 (TTY 711). Free rides to covered appointments: Bay Cities Brokerage, 877-324-8109.

Insured, but the bills are too much

Hospital discounts apply to you too

Bay Area Hospital's financial assistance covers people with insurance whose income qualifies, and Southern Coos helps underinsured patients and anyone whose medical bills top 10% of yearly income. Apply exactly the way an uninsured person would — the two hospital cards above have the numbers and the steps.

Your right to a price in writing

Federal law (the No Surprises Act) gives self-pay patients the right to a Good Faith Estimate — a written price before scheduled care. Three questions settle cost before you commit: Do you have a sliding fee scale, and what would my first visit cost at my income? Is there a charge for the intake appointment?
