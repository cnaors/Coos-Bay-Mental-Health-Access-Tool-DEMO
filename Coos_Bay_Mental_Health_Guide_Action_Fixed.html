What actually happens when you call?

988: a trained counselor answers, usually within a minute. They listen, talk things through, and help you make a plan. You can stay anonymous and hang up any time. Emergency services get involved only in the rare case where someone is in immediate danger and can't stay safe — the vast majority of calls are just a conversation.

The county line: a local crisis worker answers. They can talk you through it, set up a next-day appointment, or send the mobile crisis team out — and they know the local system, so they connect you to real follow-up.

Either way: free, and calling about someone else is normal.

Secure inpatient behavioral health care in Coos Bay. Bay Area Hospital publishes 541-269-8401 for Acute Psychiatric Services; hospital main line 541-269-8111. No one is denied care for inability to pay. · 1775 Thompson Rd

Your regular doctor or nurse practitioner can start and manage many mental health medications now. Ask them first, then transfer to a or if needed.

How to ask someone if they're thinking about suicide

Ask directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Research shows asking does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior — talking openly may actually reduce them (NIMH).

The five research-backed steps: Ask plainly and listen without judgment. Be there. Help keep them safe — ask if they have a plan, and put distance between them and anything they could use. Help them connect — call or text 988 together. Follow up — a check-in call in the days after genuinely lowers risk.

What if they refuse help?

You can't force an adult into care, and pushing hard often backfires. What works: keep the relationship open, name what you see without judgment ("I've noticed you're not sleeping — I'm worried"), and offer specific small next steps rather than "get help."

Call 988 or 541-266-6800 yourself for coaching on your exact situation. Immediate danger they won't stay safe from: 911. And your own limits are real — the NAMI family group exists because carrying this alone breaks people.

Sexual assault patient — exam rights, evidence windows, advocate, reporting
The full pathway: what Oregon guarantees the patient and what's required of you.

1. Medical needs first. Urgent care always precedes forensic care (Oregon SATF sequence).

2. Call the advocate — it's the law. ORS 147.404 requires medical staff to notify an advocate for sexual violence patients. Locally that's SAFE Project, 541-756-7000, 24/7 to the bedside. The patient chooses whether to use them. Patients also have the right to a support person (ORS 147.425).

3. Never "too late" for an exam. SATF: medical-forensic exams are possible for days after an assault (their guidance says up to ~15 days depending on circumstances). Oregon's SAVE Fund pays for SAFE Kit exams within 120 hours and non-kit exams within 168 hours. Time-critical meds: HIV nPEP within 72 hours; emergency contraception 72–120 hours.

4. Evidence preservation (if the patient may want collection): avoid showering, changing clothes, eating/drinking, urinating where feasible — and a patient who already showered can still have evidence collected. Oral swabs before food or drink.

5. The patient's rights (ORS 147.397): exam at no cost whether or not police are involved; anonymous, identity-protected kits are allowed; providers billing the SAVE Fund may not balance-bill the patient; law enforcement must keep kits at least 60 years.

6. Reporting nuance: an adult's sexual assault is NOT automatically police-reportable in Oregon — that's the patient's choice. The separate injury-reporting duty (ORS 146.750: physicians, PAs, RNs must report non-accidental physical injuries, historically framed as weapon or serious physical injuries) can still apply — follow your facility policy, and route minors under ORS 419B instead.

7. After the ED: SAFE advocates handle confidential shelter placement and safety planning. ODHS TA-DVS grants up to $3,200 for safety costs (moving, locks, deposits). Oregon DOJ's Address Confidentiality Program gives a substitute mailing address.

Protocols & standards: oregonsatf.org/medical-forensic (Oregon's medical-forensic guidelines & 2025 SAFE Kit companion) · patient-facing sequence at campusreportingoptions.org/medical-care · SAVE Fund details: doj.state.or.us

Non-fatal strangulation — treat it as a lethality red flag
The single strongest predictor that a DV patient will later be killed.

Why it changes everything: prior non-fatal strangulation is associated with roughly 7.5× higher odds of later homicide by the same partner (Glass et al. 2008, per the Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention), and 43% of women killed by partners experienced it first. Unconsciousness can occur in seconds — and life-threatening injury can be present with zero visible marks (Oregon SATF's own SAFE Kit guidance says the same).

So: every strangulation disclosure gets a medical evaluation, not just a visual check. Since August 2023, Oregon's SAVE Fund pays for strangulation (SKIT) exams even when no sexual assault occurred.

And: connect the patient with SAFE (541-756-7000) before discharge — strangulation is exactly the case where safety planning saves a life.

Clinical resources: Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention

Behavioral health crisis — holds, transport, and placement
Who can hold, where patients go, and the beds nobody mentions.

Peace officer custody (ORS 426.228): an officer may take custody on probable cause the person is dangerous to self or others and needs immediate care. Destination: the nearest OHA-approved facility — locally, Bay Area Hospital's ED — where a licensed independent practitioner must examine the person immediately. If emergency care isn't indicated, the person cannot be retained.

Director's hold (ORS 426.233): the county mental health director (Coos Health & Wellness, 541-266-6700) can direct an officer to take custody or authorize nonhospital placement. When the director reports imminent danger, the officer shall take custody. Emergency admission proceeds under ORS 426.232.

Not hold-level? Mobile crisis (541-266-6800) can respond on scene, and the county's crisis respite beds (about 5–7 day stays) take referrals from CHW clinicians and the Bay Area ED — raise it before discharge; patients rarely know to ask.

Withdrawal risk: alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be lethal — route to Adapt's medical detox (541-492-0222, Rapid Access walk-in entry), never "sleep it off at home."

Reference: OHA civil commitment · ORS 426.228

Child with suspicious injuries — Karly's Law
The 48-hour clock and Coos County's designated medical professional.

The rule (ORS 419B.022–.024): a child with suspicious physical injuries in a child-abuse investigation must be seen by a trained designated medical professional within 48 hours, and the injuries must be photographed.

"Suspicious injuries" include: burns or scalds; extensive bruising; any bruising or swelling of the head, neck, or face; any fracture under age 3; multiple fractures at any age; unconsciousness; multiple injuries of different types.

In Coos County: the DMP is Dr. Jenni DeLeon at the Kids' HOPE Center (541-266-8806, 1925 Thompson Rd, Coos Bay) — forensic interviews, exams, advocacy, no cost to families. Report first: 855-503-7233 (ODHS, 24/7) or law enforcement.

Official summary: Oregon DOJ — Karly's Law

Elder & vulnerable adult abuse — the law and the signs
Duty, timing, and what warrants the call.

The duty: ORS 124.060 — officials including physicians, nurses, EMS providers, and firefighters with reasonable cause to believe a person 65+ has been abused must report; ORS 124.065 requires the oral report immediately. ODHS extends the same intake to vulnerable adults 18+.

Where: 855-503-7233 (ODHS, 24/7) or local law enforcement. Nursing facility residents: 877-280-4555.

Signs that warrant a call (ACL)

  • Bruises, pressure marks, broken bones, burns — especially unexplained
  • Sudden withdrawal from activities, unusual depression, change in alertness
  • Bedsores, poor hygiene, unattended medical needs, unusual weight loss
  • Sudden changes in finances
  • Belittling or controlling caregiver behavior; tense caregiver relationship

One sign alone doesn't prove abuse — your job is to alert, not to verify.

Suspected child abuse — who must report, the law, and how

The law: ORS 419B.010 requires any "public or private official" with reasonable cause to believe a child has been abused to report immediately. The reporter list (ORS 419B.005) includes nurses, physicians, EMS providers and firefighters, school employees including higher-ed, therapists, clergy, attorneys, and more. Failure to report is a Class A violation.

Three things people get wrong: You don't need proof — reasonable suspicion is the standard, and investigating isn't your job. The duty is personal — telling your supervisor does not satisfy it. And it applies even if you learned of the abuse outside your job (ODHS: "regardless of whether or not you gained your knowledge… in your official capacity").

How: call 855-503-7233 (24/7) or local law enforcement — one agency is enough. Immediate danger: 911 first. In Coos County, cases route to the Kids' HOPE Center.

Warning signs that should raise concern (ODHS)

On the child's body

  • Injuries to the back, neck, ears, buttocks, or genitals — locations less typical of play
  • Any bruise on a baby under 4 months; mouth injuries in infants
  • Injuries with no explanation, or one that doesn't fit
  • Untreated medical, dental, or vision needs; poor growth without medical cause

In the child's behavior

  • Fear of a parent or not wanting to go home; cowering around a caregiver
  • Regularly running away, constant fatigue, stealing or hoarding food
  • Losing skills they had (like new bed-wetting); self-injury or talk of suicide
  • Direct statements about abuse — always taken seriously

In the caregiver's behavior

  • Disregard for the child's feelings or needs
  • Describing the child as bad, evil, worthless — or in sexual terms

ODHS caution: any single sign may or may not mean abuse — consider what's typical for that child, family, and culture. When in doubt, call and let the screeners decide.

When you hand someone this guide

Most people try the number they're given, but only about a third end up getting help — the drop-off is dead ends and discouragement. Beat those odds: pick one specific number, tell them what will happen when they call, and check back in a few days. "Did you get through?" is the highest-value question in this guide.

Cost & insurance

Cost stops more people than anything else. There's a path at every income level — and the fine print about this guide lives here too.

No insurance? Start here.

You may qualify for the Oregon Health Plan — mental health care with no copay, enrollment open year-round. Apply at one.oregon.gov or call 800-699-9075, weekdays 7–6. Free application help at ODHS offices and community partners.

Already on OHP?

Your local plan is Advanced Health. No referral needed for mental health care — call providers directly. Member services: 800-264-0014 (TTY 711). Free rides to covered appointments: Bay Cities Brokerage, 877-324-8109.

Can't get OHP, can't afford private pay?

Coos Health & Wellness, Waterfall, and Adapt all state that no one is turned away for inability to pay — sliding fees based on income. Several independent therapists offer sliding scales too (marked in the directory). It's always OK to ask "what would this cost me?" before booking.
